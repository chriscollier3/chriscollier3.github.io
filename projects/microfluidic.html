01 — Engineering Research & Development

Microfluidic Glass-Elastomer
Membrane (GEM) Oxygenator

A PDMS-coated glass plate membrane device for blood gas exchange — designed, fabricated, and validated at the BRITE Lab, Nemours Children's Health, under PI Dr. David Blauvelt, MD.

Lab BRITE Lab, Nemours Children's Health
PI Dr. David Blauvelt, MD
Device Type Flat Plate Membrane Oxygenator
Membrane PDMS-coated glass
CAD OnShape
Target Scale 32-channel manifold device

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) is an advanced therapy for cardiac and respiratory support that mimics alveolar gas exchange outside the body. Current commercial oxygenators carry significant limitations — large priming volumes, increased anticoagulation requirements, and uncontrolled internal flow patterns that can lead to hemolysis. These issues are particularly acute in pediatric and neonatal populations, where device size and blood-contacting surface area must be minimized.

Prior lab work developed a silicon PDMS flat-plate oxygenator, but was limited to channel heights of ~500 µm due to 3D printing constraints and failed sealing above 15 psi. The GEM Oxygenator addresses both limitations: replacing the silicon PDMS membrane substrate with a glass slide produces a thinner, more dimensionally stable membrane, and a new polycarbonate flow cell with a mechanically fastened assembly achieves reliable, reproducible sealing. The ultimate goal is a reproducible single-channel device scalable into a 32-channel blood distribution manifold for clinical-scale gas exchange.

My work spans the full development cycle — CAD modeling in OnShape, fabrication and assembly, channel height characterization, manifold design and sealing iteration, and a structured V&V testing framework to validate device integrity before advancing to biological testing.

Design & Development
Breakdown

01

Flow Cell Design & Sealing

The polycarbonate flow cell was modeled in OnShape, defining the blood-side channel geometry, gasket seating surfaces, and fastener pattern. Viton cord stock serves a dual purpose as both the channel-defining boundary and the lateral seal — a key design feature that simplifies assembly while providing consistent channel geometry and compression sealing in a single component.

Two sealing approaches were evaluated: a 302-3M epoxy with C-clamp assembly and a mechanically fastened design using 8 Torx screws driven with a calibrated torque screwdriver. The torqued screw design proved superior — it eliminated the variability of adhesive cure and clamp placement, and enabled the key finding that torque directly and predictably controls channel height. The epoxy approach produced a mean channel height of ~229 µm with high variability; the torqued screw design at 80 cN·m produced a consistent ~144 µm, within the target range of 125–175 µm.

02

PDMS Membrane Fabrication

The gas transfer membrane is a thin PDMS film cast on a glass slide substrate, produced via spin coating. PDMS provides high gas permeability for O₂ and CO₂ transfer while maintaining biocompatibility. The glass backing provides dimensional stability, a flat bonding surface for assembly into the flow cell, and eliminates the channel height variability seen with purely PDMS substrates in prior devices. The membrane is handled with care during assembly to avoid contamination or deformation of the gas transfer surface.

03

Channel Height Characterization

Controlling channel height is critical — it governs flow resistance, residence time, and gas transfer efficiency. Channel height was measured using two methods: micrometer measurement of assembled devices and CT scan verification of the internal blood channel geometry. Results confirmed that applied torque directly determines channel height in the torqued screw design. At 80 cN·m, devices consistently produced channel heights of ~144 µm, within the 125–175 µm target window. Over-torquing at 100 cN·m collapsed the channel to ~70 µm, confirming that assembly protocol compliance is critical to reproducibility.

04

Manifold Design & Sealing

Scaling the single-channel device to a 32-channel system requires a blood distribution manifold — a component that connects to the flow cell ports and distributes blood flow across all channels in parallel. The manifold is designed in OnShape and printed on a Formlabs SLA resin printer for dimensional accuracy. Three manifold sealing iterations were developed and tested: an unsealed baseline, adhesive grooves, and a grooves-plus-O-ring face seal as the current design.

O-ring sizing followed the Parker O-Ring Handbook, using perimeter-matched stretch-fit logic to select the correct O-ring for each manifold runner port. The grooves-plus-O-ring approach is currently undergoing pressure hold testing to confirm seal integrity before integration with the full flow cell assembly. This manifold work is a key step toward the 32-channel device goal.

05

Verification & Validation Testing

A structured V&V protocol was developed to characterize device integrity at each stage of development. External leak testing uses a pressure hold protocol — pressurizing the assembled device and monitoring for pressure decay — and a submersion test to visually confirm housing seal integrity. Trans-membrane integrity is evaluated via dye perfusion, confirming that blood and gas compartments remain fully separated across the PDMS membrane. Bubble point testing characterizes the membrane's defect threshold pressure. The manifold assembly has also been pressure hold tested at 30 psi to evaluate the O-ring seal under physiologically relevant loading. Results are documented photographically at each stage.
